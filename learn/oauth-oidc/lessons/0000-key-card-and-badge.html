Lesson 0 · Foundations

Key card and badge

OAuth and OIDC solve two different problems. OAuth lets an app use something on your behalf. OIDC tells the app who you are. Keep these apart and most auth discussions get simple.

The problem: sharing your password

A reporting app wants to read your Google Calendar. Before OAuth, the only way was to give the app your Google password. The OAuth spec lists what goes wrongRFC 6749 §1:

  • The app stores your password, often in plain text.
  • The app gets all your access, for as long as it likes.
  • You cannot take access away from one app without changing your password for every app.
  • If the app is hacked, your password and all your data are gone.

OAuth: a key card instead of a password

Think of a hotel. Reception checks your passport once and gives you a key card. The door does not care who you are. It only checks that the card opens this room, today.

The app carries the key card but does not read it. The spec says an access token is "usually opaque to the client"RFC 6749 §1.4. The app has no reliable way to learn your name from it.

RememberOAuth is about access: may this app do this thing? It is not about who the user is.

OIDC: a badge for the app

Apps wanted "Log in with Google". Many misused OAuth for it: "I got a key card, so this must be Anna." That is unsafe, because the key card was never a statement to the app about who the user isoauth.net: User authentication with OAuth 2.0.

OpenID Connect is a thin identity layer on top of OAuthOIDC Core §1. Next to the key card, reception now also gives the app a signed badge: "This is Anna. Google checked her at 08:48. This badge is for ReportApp." That badge is the ID tokenOIDC Core §2.

In our design

Option A-5 (Entra login) gives the connector a key card made for the BSL API. That is the normal shape. Option A-3 shows Google's badge at the API's door. It works in v1 because only our own connector exists, but it is the shortcut. Lesson 1 shows exactly why.

Check yourself

1. In the hotel picture, what is the ID token?

The ID token is the signed badge. It tells the app who the user is. The key card is the access token.

2. Which problem does OAuth solve first?

OAuth replaces password sharing with limited, short-lived, revocable tokens. Finding the user's email is OIDC's job.

3. Who is supposed to read the access token?

The API checks the access token. For the app it is opaque: something to carry, not to read.

4. An app says: "I got an access token, so the user is Anna." What is wrong?

This is the classic misuse that OIDC was made to fix. Identity comes from the ID token, which is made for the app.

5. Why is giving an app your password bad? Pick the best reason.

With a shared password, the only way to stop one app is to change the password, which breaks every other app too. The app also gets all your access.

Say it without looking

OAuth vs OIDC in one sentence each.
OAuth gives an app a key card (access token) to use an API on your behalf. OIDC adds a badge (ID token) that tells the app who you are.
Name three things that go wrong when you give an app your password.
It gets all your access; you cannot revoke only that app; it may store the password badly; a hack of the app leaks your password.
